Each activity below is tagged with its AI rule, from AI: expected in Create / Scaffold to No AI in Understand & Refine and the Knowledge Check. The AI Use Guide explains each one.

A heat wave is forecast for Thursday: 38 °C outside by mid-afternoon. Floor 3's twin from AIC 1 tracks four rooms, two HVAC units and the people in them. Facilities wants an agent that adjusts setpoints during the afternoon, and wants to know, before Thursday, what happens if a unit fails at the peak. Nobody is going to switch off a real HVAC unit on a 38 °C day to find out. The twin can, a few hundred times.

Week 7 builds a what-if sweep on a SimPy model of the floor with an AI assistant's help (outside temperature × HVAC capacity × occupancy), and runs an ADK agent that reads the twin and changes setpoints over MQTT, through a safety check that currently allows everything. Then you try to make it act unsafely. Week 8 is the harder half: explaining one mechanism line by line, and writing the safety envelope yourself, from your own sweep results, by hand. Week 8 also reviews the whole architecture across all 4 AICs.

Anchoring analogy

A flight simulator lets a pilot lose an engine on take-off a hundred times, so the first real one is a procedure rather than a surprise. The procedure also says what the pilot may do alone and when to call it in. What-if testing is the twin's flight simulator, and the safety envelope is its procedure. (A procedure written from a simulator that never modeled icing is no help in icing.)

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

Lecture: testing in the twin before acting in the world

≈ 60 min3 short partsCO5

Three short segments: read the notes, then try the quick check before moving on. The notes cover everything you need; the "Read more" links are optional. If something stays fuzzy, note it: that's exactly what your Post Mortem is for.

Part 1 — What-if testing: ask the twin first ≈ 20 min

A what-if scenario is a question you ask the twin instead of the building: what if it's 38 °C outside, a unit fails and the floor is full? A sweep asks it systematically: every combination of a few factors, each over a few values, run in simulation. The output is a map of where the system is fine, where it's degraded and where it fails.

This AIC uses SimPy, a discrete-event simulation library: rooms gain heat from outside and from people, HVAC units remove it up to their capacity, and events (a unit failing at 14:00, a meeting ending at 15:00) happen at set times. Each run takes a fraction of a second, so a sweep of 4 temperatures × 4 capacities × 3 occupancies, with 10 random seeds each, is 480 runs and a few minutes on CPU.

Worked example

Tested one at a time, a 38 °C afternoon is fine, one failed unit is fine and a full floor is fine. Together, room r2 stays above 27 °C for three hours, because the remaining unit serves r2 last. The single-scenario tests all passed. Real incidents are usually combinations, which is why you sweep rather than spot-check.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Testing failures one at a time. The worst cases are combinations. Sweep the factors together.
  • Reading a sweep without its seeds. One run per cell can be lucky. Run several seeds and report the worst, not just the average.
  • Forgetting the twin's own limits. A sweep can only find failures the model can represent. A fire alarm or a jammed damper isn't in this one.
Quick check

Your sweep has three factors at four values each. A colleague wants to add a fourth factor, "a sensor stuck at its last reading". How many runs does that add, and why might it be the most important factor of all (AIC 1)?

Part 2 — From test results to a safety envelope ≈ 20 min

A safety envelope is the set of states and actions your tests showed to be safe, written as rules the agent can't break. Three kinds of rule cover most of it:

  • State limits: a setpoint between 20 and 26 °C, never outside what the sweep tested.
  • Rate limits: no more than one change per room every 10 minutes, and no step bigger than 2 °C. Many failures are an agent chasing noise.
  • Stop-and-alert conditions: when the twin no longer matches the building (a sensor disagrees with the prediction by more than 3 °C) or the building is in a state the sweep showed fails, the agent stops acting and a person is told.

The envelope sits outside the agent, in code, where the agent's reasoning can't talk its way past it. In ADK, a before_tool_callback runs before every tool call and can refuse it:

def check_envelope(tool, args, tool_context):        # runs before EVERY tool call
    if tool.name != "set_setpoint":
        return None                                  # None = allow the call
    verdict, reason = envelope.check(args, twin.state())
    log_action(tool.name, args, verdict, reason)     # logged whether allowed or not
    if verdict != "allow":
        return {"status": verdict, "reason": reason} # a dict = the tool never runs

root_agent = Agent(..., tools=[read_twin, set_setpoint],
                   before_tool_callback=check_envelope)

One honest limit: the envelope came from the twin's model, so it inherits that model's assumptions. If the model is wrong, or a sensor feeding it is lying, the envelope can be wrong in exactly the same place.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Putting the limits in the agent's instruction. An instruction is a request; a callback is a rule. Safety goes in code.
  • Writing limits that aren't from your tests. Every number in the envelope should point to a sweep result. If you didn't test it, it isn't inside the envelope.
  • Only checking the action. A safe action on a wrong state is still unsafe. Check that the twin still matches the building, too.
Quick check

An envelope blocks 40% of the agent's proposals on a normal afternoon. Is that a safe envelope or a useless one? What would you look at to decide?

Part 3 — Safe autonomous decisions, and who answers for them ≈ 20 min

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. For each action, decide how much of it the twin may do alone, and write that decision down:

LevelWhat the twin doesFits
AdviseShows a recommendation; a person actsNew twins, irreversible actions
Act with approvalProposes; a person confirms each actionRare, high-impact actions (restarting a unit)
Act inside the envelopeActs alone within tested limits; stops and alerts outside themFrequent, small, reversible actions (a setpoint ±1 °C)
Act freelyNo envelopeAlmost never, for a physical system

When something goes wrong anyway, the action log is how you find out which layer failed: a stuck sensor (AIC 1), a wrong model (AIC 2), a simulator blind spot (AIC 3), a missing envelope rule or a bad agent decision (AIC 4). Each entry needs the inputs the agent saw, the twin's state, the proposed action, the envelope's verdict and the reason, with a timestamp. Without them, accountability is guesswork.

Worked example

r2 overheats on Thursday. The log shows the agent raised r2's setpoint to 25 °C at 14:10, the envelope allowed it, and the twin's state said r2 was at 22 °C. r2's sensor had been stuck at 22 °C since 13:40. The agent and the envelope both did what they were built to do; the missing piece was the stuck-sensor check from AIC 1, wired into the stop-and-alert rule.

Read more (optional): SimPy documentation · Google ADK, Callbacks · NIST, AI Risk Management Framework · Amodei et al., Concrete problems in AI safety (2016)

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Logging only allowed actions. Blocked proposals show what the agent was trying to do. Log every proposal and its verdict.
  • "The agent is accurate, so it can act freely." Accuracy on tested situations says little about untested ones. The envelope is for those.
  • No route to a person. A stop-and-alert rule that stops without alerting anyone just leaves the room hot.
Quick check

Which actions in your Term Project should never be autonomous, at any level of model accuracy? Say why for each.

A question asked of the twin instead of the physical system, such as a failure under given conditions.
Sweep
Running every combination of a few factors, each at a few values, to map where the system fails.
Combined failure
Two or more conditions that are each survivable alone and fail together.

The states and actions tests showed to be safe, enforced as rules the agent can't break.

Concept Debate B · Integration — connecting all 4 AICs

CD08 · Discussion board · 3 stagesAI: think, don't write
This week's question

Which is the bigger risk in a deployed digital twin: an inaccurate model, or an accurate model allowed to act on its own?

You'll argue a side you're assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize. Nobody gets to post "I agree" — by Stage 3 everyone has argued both sides.

Side A — An inaccurate modelGroup 1 this debate

A twin whose model is wrong is the bigger risk, even if it only advises.

  • Every layer inherits the model's errors: the PINN's parameters, the simulator's ranges and the envelope derived from them (AIC 2–4).
  • People stop checking a twin that is usually right, so its advice becomes a decision anyway.
  • A stuck sensor upstream (AIC 1) makes even the best model wrong, quietly.

Side B — AutonomyGroup 2 this debate

An accurate model allowed to act on its own is the bigger risk.

  • Accurate models still meet situations outside anything they were tested on.
  • Autonomy removes the person who would have noticed the mismatch.
  • A wrong forecast is a number on a dashboard; a wrong action is physical, and may not be reversible.
Stage 1 · by Tue

Initial position

Argue your assigned side with one specific claim and one piece of evidence: a run you did, a reading, a real-world example or a simulator setting. Not a topic summary.
Stage 2 · by Thu

Argue the opposite

Before replying to anyone, post the strongest version of the side you didn't hold.
Stage 3 · by Sun

Synthesis

Drop advocacy. State what you'll do in your project, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post. It becomes a Week 9 planning decision.
See a weak vs. strong example
Weak synthesis"Both are risky, so you should be careful with both."
Strong synthesis"Mei's lying-sensor run showed the envelope passing an unsafe setpoint because the twin believed r2 was cool, so an inaccurate model defeats the envelope built on it. But Tomas's point stands: in the scripted heat wave, the agent's model was right and it still changed setpoints twelve times in an hour until the rate limit stopped it. For my project: the agent may only slow the conveyor, never stop or restart it, and it stops and alerts whenever the jaw sensor and the PINN disagree by more than 5 °C."

Grading: Participation — all three stages posted on time, the last one a reply to a classmate (60%) · Writing — a specific claim with evidence, a genuine best case for the other side, and a synthesis that cites a classmate (40%). See a worked example: four students through one debate, with a timeline → A reply that only affirms agreement without citing what it agrees with doesn't meet the Writing criterion.
