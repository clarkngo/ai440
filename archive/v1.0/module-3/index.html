Simulation, Synthetic Data & Sim-to-Real

Training a robot arm on the real thing is slow, expensive and sometimes dangerous, so physical AI is trained in simulation. The catch is that the simulator is never quite the real world. Build a simulated work cell and the synthetic data it produces, train a pushing policy with and without randomized physics, and measure how each one holds up on worlds it never saw. What's the HOS?

HOS 3 (Hands-On Skill) is this module's main build and 10% of your course grade. It has four stages across the two weeks: Create / Scaffold and Evaluate in Week 5, then Analyze and Understand & Refine in Week 6. All four go in one GitHub Classroom repo, and that repo is your submission: there's nothing to upload in Brightspace, and your last push before the end of Week 6 is what counts. The Muddiest Point, Concept Debate, Application Challenge and Knowledge Check are graded separately.

A packaging cell has a small arm that pushes cartons onto a conveyor. The cartons change: some are light and slide easily, some are heavy, some are damp and grip the table. Training the push on the real arm would take days of supervised runs and a few broken cartons. In a simulator it takes minutes, and you can try the jams and the slippery cartons as often as you like. But a policy that only ever practiced on one simulated carton can fail on the first real one that's a bit different. That difference is the sim-to-real gap.

Week 5 builds the simulated cell in MuJoCo, exports it to OpenUSD, renders labeled synthetic images of its parts with BlenderProc, and, with an AI assistant's help, trains two pushing policies with Stable-Baselines3 on CPU: one in a fixed world and one with randomized friction and mass. Then you test both on held-out worlds, which stand in for reality. Week 6 is the harder half: explaining one mechanism of the setup line by line, and randomizing one more thing reality varies, by hand.

Anchoring analogy

A driving student who only ever practiced in one car, on one dry road, in the afternoon, may pass the test in that car and struggle in the rain in someone else's. One who practiced in several cars, wet and dry, morning and night, has learned to drive rather than to drive that car. Domain randomization gives a policy many cars. (Practicing on ice, in a bus, at night, all at once, is too much, and they may never learn at all.)

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Construct a physically accurate simulated work cell and describe it in OpenUSD. CO4

Lecture: training in simulation, and crossing the gap

≈ 60 min3 short partsCO4

Three short segments: read the notes, then try the quick check before moving on. The notes cover everything you need; the "Read more" links are optional. If something stays fuzzy, that's exactly what your Muddiest Point is for.

Part 1 — Why simulate, and the sim-to-real gap ≈ 20 min

Simulation gives physical AI three things the real world can't: speed (thousands of practice episodes an hour), safety (the arm can crash, the carton can fall, nothing breaks) and perfect labels (the simulator knows exactly where every part is, so images label themselves). It also lets you practice the rare events, such as a jam or a dropped part, as often as the common ones.

The price is the sim-to-real gap: every way the simulator differs from reality. Physics parameters are only estimates (friction, mass, motor strength); contact is approximated; sensors in reality are noisy and late; and rendered images are cleaner than camera frames. A policy can learn to exploit any of these, so it's excellent in the simulator and not in the cell.

Worked example

A pushing policy trained on one carton with friction 0.8 succeeds 98% of the time on that carton. On a damp carton with friction 1.3 it succeeds 41% of the time: it pushes at the angle that worked when the carton slid easily, and the carton rotates off the target. Nothing about the policy changed. Only the world did.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Testing on the training world. Success there says the policy learned the simulator. Test on worlds it never saw.
  • Assuming more realism always helps. Realism costs compute, and on two CPU cores it's a budget. Spend it on what the task actually depends on.
  • Treating the simulator as truth. Its parameters came from a spec sheet or a guess. Write down where each one came from.
Quick check

Name three ways the simulated cell in this HOS differs from a real packaging cell. Which one would you expect to hurt the pushing policy most, and why?

Part 2 — Building the simulated world ≈ 20 min

Three tools, three jobs. MuJoCo is the physics engine: bodies, joints, masses, friction and contact, described in an XML file (MJCF) and stepped hundreds of times a second. OpenUSD (Universal Scene Description) is a shared scene format: one description of the cell that renderers, viewers and other simulators can all read, so the cell isn't locked into one tool. BlenderProc renders images of a scene with Blender and writes the labels (bounding boxes and masks) for every image, for free.

<!-- simulation/cell.xml: the carton the arm pushes -->
<body name="carton" pos="0.40 0 0.03">
  <freejoint/>
  <geom name="carton_geom" type="box" size="0.03 0.03 0.03"
        mass="0.20" friction="0.8 0.005 0.0001"/>
</body>

Physically accurate means the numbers are right: a real mass, a measured friction coefficient, a motor strength from the spec. Photorealistic means the images look like camera frames: real materials, lighting and noise. They're separate dials. A pushing policy reads positions, not pixels, so it needs accurate physics and no images at all. A pick detector needs images, and it's often fine with images that are varied rather than beautiful.

To export the cell to OpenUSD, MuJoCo's Python package includes a USD exporter (it needs the free usd-core package). The result is a text .usda file you can open and read.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Changing the XML file every episode. Reloading the model is slow. Change the loaded model's arrays (such as model.geom_friction) directly.
  • Rendering more than you need. 200 images at 256 × 256 can teach a small detector; 2,000 at 1080p will take your Codespace all night.
  • Trusting labels you haven't looked at. A box can be labeled while hidden behind the arm. Draw the labels over a few images and check.
Quick check

A detector trained on your rendered images fails on images with motion blur. Is that a physics accuracy problem, a photorealism problem or a randomization problem? What would you change?

Part 3 — Domain randomization and measuring the gap ≈ 20 min

You can't make the simulator match reality exactly, so domain randomization stops trying: each training episode draws new friction, mass, lighting or textures from a range, so the policy can't rely on any one value. If the real world falls inside the ranges, it looks to the policy like one more variation. The ranges are the design choice. Too narrow, and reality falls outside them. Too wide, and the task becomes unlearnable, or the policy learns something cautious that works everywhere and well nowhere.

ApproachWhat it doesWhen it fits
Domain randomizationTrain across a range of worldsYou know roughly what varies, and not the exact values.
System identificationMeasure the real parameters and set them in the simulatorYou can measure the real system, and it doesn't change much.
Fine-tuning on real dataTrain in simulation, finish on a little real dataA few safe real runs are possible.

Without real hardware, this course measures the gap with held-out worlds: test worlds whose physics was altered in ways the policy never trained on, some inside the training ranges and some outside. They stand in for reality, with one honest limit: they come from the same simulator, so they share its blind spots.

Worked example

The policy trained with friction randomized from 0.6 to 1.2 succeeds 88% of the time on the damp carton (1.3) and 93% on its home world, five points below the fixed policy there. Widened to 0.1–3.0, training never converges: the same push can't work on ice and on rubber, so the policy learns to barely move.

Read more (optional): Tobin et al., Domain randomization for transferring deep neural networks from simulation to the real world (2017) · Peng et al., Sim-to-real transfer of robotic control with dynamics randomization (2018) · MuJoCo documentation · Introduction to OpenUSD · BlenderProc · Stable-Baselines3 documentation

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Choosing ranges with no reason. Write down where each range came from: a spec sheet, a measurement, or a guess you'll test.
  • Reporting only the average over all test worlds. One world at 20% matters more than an average of 85%. Report each world.
  • Calling held-out worlds "real". They're a stand-in. Say what reality could still do that your simulator can't.
Quick check

Your randomized policy beats the fixed one on every held-out world but loses five points on its home world. When is that trade worth it, and when isn't it?

▶ Rehearse before the build: Open the Sim-to-Real Lab. Train in a fixed world, test on held-out worlds, then widen the friction range one step at a time.

    Side A — A substituteGroup 1 this HOS

    With enough randomization, simulation can replace real data.

    • Real data is slow, expensive and sometimes dangerous to collect.
    • Simulators give perfect labels and as many rare events as you need.
    • Randomized policies already transfer to worlds they never saw (your held-out table).

    Side B — A supplement onlyGroup 2 this HOS

    Real data has to anchor and test it.

    • Every simulator has blind spots its own test worlds share.
    • Only real data can show the gap you're actually facing.
    • A small real set can tell you which randomization ranges were wrong.
