Physics-Informed Neural Networks

A twin has to predict where no sensor is: inside a wall, between two probes, an hour from now. A plain neural network fits the readings and can do anything in the gaps. A physics-informed neural network (PINN) adds the heat equation to its training loss, so breaking physics costs as much as missing data. Built in Week 3, explained and extended by hand in Week 4.

What's the HOS?

HOS 2 (Hands-On Skill) is this module's main build and 10% of your course grade. It has four stages across the two weeks: Create / Scaffold and Evaluate in Week 3, then Analyze and Understand & Refine in Week 4. All four go in one GitHub Classroom repo, and that repo is your submission: there's nothing to upload in Brightspace, and your last push before the end of Week 4 is what counts. The Muddiest Point, Concept Debate, Application Challenge and Knowledge Check are graded separately.

Floor 3's exterior wall has three temperature sensors buried in it. On the first night of a cold snap the outside surface drops to −5 °C, and facilities wants to know how cold the inner layers will get by morning, because a cold inner layer means condensation and mold. Three sensors and three hours of readings don't answer that. A model has to fill the gaps between the sensors and extrapolate nine hours past the data, and a model that only knows the data has no reason to stay physical while it does.

Week 3 trains a PINN in plain PyTorch on CPU with an AI assistant's help, compares it with a data-only network on the same sparse, noisy readings, and then tries to make it fail: wrong physics, too little physics, too much. Week 4 is the harder half: explaining one mechanism of the loss line by line, and making the wall's thermal diffusivity a parameter the PINN learns from the data, by hand.

Anchoring analogy

Two students extrapolate a thrown ball's path from three photos. One draws the smoothest curve through the dots, and it has the ball curving back up. The other knows gravity, so every curve they consider is a parabola. The second needs fewer photos and never draws something impossible. A PINN is the second student; the physics term in its loss is knowing gravity. (And if they think they're on the Moon, they'll be confidently wrong.)

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Apply a PINN so a twin's predictions obey a physical law, here heat flow, between and beyond the sensors. CO2
  2. Explain each term of a PINN's loss: data, physics residual and boundary conditions. CO2
  3. Compare a PINN and a data-only network on sparse, noisy data, inside and outside the data, with evidence. CO2
  4. Tune the balance (λ) between fitting the data and obeying the physics, and explain both extremes. CO2
  5. Identify when a PINN is confidently wrong, and make a physical parameter learnable. CO2

Lecture: making predictions obey physics

≈ 60 min3 short partsCO2

Three short segments: read the notes, then try the quick check before moving on. The notes cover everything you need; the "Read more" links are optional. If something stays fuzzy, that's exactly what your Muddiest Point is for.

Part 1 — Why data-only models break physics ≈ 20 min

With a few noisy readings, a flexible model fits the noise and invents wiggles between the points. Outside the data it extrapolates however its shape happens to continue. For a twin that's dangerous, because the twin is consulted exactly in those gaps: inside the wall where there's no sensor, and tomorrow morning, where there's no data yet.

Physics knows things the data can't show. Heat in a wall with no heat source obeys the heat equation, ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x², where α is the material's thermal diffusivity. One consequence, the maximum principle: no point inside the wall gets colder than the coldest thing it started from or is held at, here the −5 °C outside surface, or warmer than the warmest. A prediction of −8 °C inside the wall isn't just inaccurate. It's impossible.

Worked example

A data-only network trained on three hours of readings matches every sensor within 0.3 °C. Asked about hour nine, it predicts the middle of the wall at −11 °C, colder than the outside air, because the trend it learned was "dropping" and nothing told it to stop. The PINN's prediction levels off at the straight-line profile between 21 °C and −5 °C, which is where heat flow says a wall settles.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Judging a model by its error at the sensors. Both models do well there. Judge them between the sensors and past the data, which is where the twin is used.
  • "More data will fix it." Not for tomorrow morning: there is no data from the future. Physics is the only information you have there.
  • Forgetting the law's assumptions. The heat equation here assumes one material, no heat source and heat flowing only through the wall. A wet patch or an air gap breaks them.
Quick check

Your model predicts a room warming up after its heater turns off, on a cold night. Which law did it break, and why would collecting more readings at that moment help less than you'd think?

Part 2 — The PINN loss ≈ 20 min

A PINN is an ordinary neural network u(x, t) with an unusual loss. Automatic differentiation gives the exact derivatives of the network's output with respect to its inputs, so you can measure how badly the network breaks the heat equation at any point, with or without a reading there:

def residual(model, x, t):                      # PHYSICS: zero where the law holds
    u    = model(x, t)                          # x and t have requires_grad=True
    u_t  = grad(u, t)                           # du/dt   via torch.autograd.grad
    u_x  = grad(u, x)
    u_xx = grad(u_x, x)                         # d2u/dx2
    return u_t - ALPHA * u_xx

loss = mse(model(x_data, t_data), u_data)                      # DATA: fit the readings
     + LAMBDA * mean(residual(model, x_col, t_col) ** 2)       # PHYSICS: at random points
     + mse(model(0, t_b), T_IN) + mse(model(L, t_b), T_OUT)    # BOUNDARIES: known surfaces

The collocation points (x_col, t_col) are random points over the whole wall and the whole twelve hours, nowhere near a sensor. That's the point: the data term teaches the network what was measured, and the physics term teaches it how everything else must behave. λ balances them. Too small, and you have a data-only network again. Too large, and the physics wins even when the data says your physics is wrong.

The same pattern covers the other laws in Outcome CO2: for a falling object the residual is x″ + g, and for a damped spring with friction it's m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Computing the physics term only at the sensors. Then it adds almost nothing; the data already pins those points. Sample collocation points everywhere you need predictions.
  • Forgetting create_graph=True. Without it you can't take the second derivative, and the residual silently uses the wrong value or errors out.
  • Mixing units. With x in metres, t in seconds and u in °C, the terms differ by orders of magnitude. Scale x and t to [0, 1] and rescale α to match.
Quick check

Set λ to 0. What is your PINN now, exactly? Then set λ to 1,000. What does the network ignore, and how would the loss history show it?

Part 3 — When PINNs help, and when they don't ≈ 20 min

PINNs shine with sparse, noisy data and a trusted equation: a few sensors, a known material, and questions about places and times the sensors don't cover. They struggle when the equation is wrong or incomplete, when the solution has sharp fronts, and when training takes longer than the decision allows. On two CPU cores this HOS's PINN trains in a few minutes; a 3-D building would not.

SituationBetter choiceWhy
Three sensors, known material, predict tomorrowPINNPhysics fills the gaps the data leaves.
Years of data, no governing law (foot traffic)Data-only modelThere's no physics to add.
A known equation and no data at allClassical solverSolve the equation directly; a network adds nothing.
Known law, unknown parameter (the wall's real α)PINN with a learnable parameterThe data corrects the parameter; the law keeps the shape. Your Week 4 build.
Worked example

The spec sheet says the wall's diffusivity is α. The real wall, a bit damp, conducts 50% better. The PINN with a heavy λ trusts the spec sheet over its own nine sensor readings, and predicts the inner layer will stay comfortable until morning. It's off by four degrees, and it looks just as smooth and confident as when it was right.

After plain PyTorch, you'll read the same problem written in DeepXDE, a PINN library, and map its pieces back to your own loss, the way you'd check any framework.

Read more (optional): Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis, Physics-informed neural networks (2019), the paper that named them · DeepXDE documentation · PyTorch, Automatic differentiation with torch.autograd

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Treating smooth as correct. A wrong-parameter PINN is perfectly smooth. Compare it with the readings it was given, and watch the data term in the loss history.
  • Turning λ up when the model and data disagree. That hides the disagreement. A persistent gap means the physics or its parameters need fixing.
  • Reaching for a PINN because it sounds advanced. No law, or no data gaps, means no reason for one (this week's Application Challenge).
Quick check

For a twin of a server room, with twelve temperature sensors and a CRAC unit whose airflow nobody has measured, would you use a PINN, a data-only model or a classical solver? What would change your answer?

▶ Rehearse before the build: Open the Wall Heat PINN Simulator. Move the time slider past the data, set λ to 0, then set the assumed diffusivity wrong and turn λ up.

New words this week (7)
PINN
A neural network trained with the governing physical equation in its loss.
Physics residual
How far the network's prediction is from satisfying the equation at a point.
Collocation points
Random points where the physics term is computed, with or without data there.
λ (lambda)
The weight that balances fitting the data against obeying the physics.
Boundary and initial conditions
Known values at the edges of space or the start of time that the solution must match.
Thermal diffusivity (α)
How quickly a material spreads heat; the one parameter of the heat equation here.
Maximum principle
With no heat source, no point gets hotter or colder than its boundaries and starting values.

    Side A — Trust the physicsGroup 2 this HOS
