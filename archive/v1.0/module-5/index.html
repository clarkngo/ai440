Term Project: Plan, Build & Present

All four HOS, combined into one functional digital twin for a targeted use case, built on your own or in a group of up to three. Phases 1 and 2 set it up; Phase 3 (Weeks 8–10) reviews the architecture in Week 8, then builds, freezes, rehearses, and ends with the final system, report, presentation and demo video.

Codespaces and free tiers only

Like every module, your project runs in GitHub Codespaces on CPU and uses only free tools and free tiers: MQTT, InfluxDB and Grafana in Docker, PyTorch on CPU, MuJoCo, SimPy, and Google ADK with a free Gemini key if your twin uses an agent. Anything that needs a GPU, a paid key or a billing account is out of scope. A grader must be able to open your repo in a Codespace and run it from the README.

What Weeks 9–10 are for

20% of courseOutcomes CO1–CO6Solo or group

The Term Project combines all 4 HOS into one functional digital twin for a targeted use case, such as a smart building, a drone or a manufacturing cell, that demonstrates real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making (CO6): a real-time data pipeline that keeps the virtual model in sync with its sensors (HOS 1), a physics-informed model of how the system behaves (HOS 2), a simulation environment and synthetic data (HOS 3), and what-if testing with safe autonomous decisions (HOS 4). You've built every piece once already. The work now is synthesis: deciding how the pieces fit, building the combined version, and proving where it can and can't be trusted.

The project runs all term in three phases. By the end of Week 7 you have locked your scope (Phase 1) and have a rough working version (Phase 2). Phase 3 starts with the Week 8 architecture review, then a backlog in Week 9, then builds. Week 10 finishes, freezes, rehearses, and presents.

Day 1: map your twin

Physical · virtual · connection

Draw the three components, from your Week 8 review drawing, and list every decision anything in the system makes. For each decision, choose who makes it, and write down why. Autonomy is allowed only where your what-if tests support it.

A decision is made by…WhenExample, from a packaging cell
The twin, inside its envelopeIt's frequent, small and reversible, and your what-ifs tested it.Slow the conveyor 10% when the sealer jaw is predicted to run cold.
Deterministic codeA fixed rule does the job, with no model needed.Stop the line the instant the light curtain is broken.
A personIt's rare, high-impact or irreversible, or the twin can't be trusted right now.Restart the line after an emergency stop.

It's the Module 1 question again, now for your own system: where is the arrow back, and what is allowed to travel along it?

Backlog and Sprint 1

Scrum-liteKeep it brief

Turn each part of your twin map into a user story — "As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]" — and tag each with the HOS it depends on. Then mark a small, realistic subset as Sprint 1: what you can actually finish in the rest of Week 9 and Week 10. Everything else stays backlog, not a promise. Your list stays in this browser; copy it into your PM tool when it's ready.

Depends on:

Coverage

Sprint 1 should touch at least two HOS end to end this week, and all four by the Week 10 demo.

Litmus test for each HOS

For every HOS, can you point to a specific part of your twin and say why it's really that HOS? "This is HOS 2: the jaw's heat equation is in the loss, and it beats the data-only model between the two sensors" — not "we used a PINN somewhere." If a HOS only has a token mention, the Architecture criterion and your final report will show it.

Roles and a project management tool

Day 1–2

Working solo? You own every area, so use the split below as a checklist and plan your sprints to cover each one; the PM tool still keeps you honest. In a group (up to three; four with instructor approval): assign an owner per area, not per week. One workable split: sensors & pipeline (HOS 1), models (HOS 2, and HOS 3's simulation), and what-if tests, envelope & demo (HOS 4, plus owning the demo script). Everyone still commits code: in a group, the Git Workflow & Collaboration criterion looks for commits from every member, not one big push before the deadline. Solo, it looks for steady commits across the term.

Build: same Create → Evaluate habit, bigger scope

Rest of Week 9 → Week 10
  • Start from your HOS builds. They're the skeleton: the HOS 1 stack and twin, the HOS 2 model, the HOS 3 simulation, the HOS 4 envelope. Adapt them to your system; don't start over.
  • Scaffold with an AI assistant, then judge it. One story at a time, the same way each HOS's Create stage worked, and read every diff. The AI Use Guide covers what to disclose in your README's AI Tool Disclosure.
  • Get the loop working first: a sensor reading → the twin → a prediction → one decision → an action or an alert, before widening any part.
  • Run your what-ifs early. Your envelope's limits should point to sweep results, and the sweep should include at least one combined failure.
  • Keep the action log on. The demo needs one real failure from development: what broke, how the log or a test showed it, and what fixed it.
  • Mind the Codespace. Two cores and a few GB of memory: keep models small, training short and Docker services few. A grader will run it there.
  • Rehearse in the simulators when you're unsure how a layer should behave: 1 · 2 · 3 · 4.

Standups

Async is fine

Brief, regular check-ins once building starts — a group chat is fine. Solo? Write the same three lines to yourself, or post them to your instructor. The point is to catch "I've been stuck for three days" before demo week, not during it.

Standup — [date] — [name]
Did:      the jaw PINN now reads live temps from InfluxDB (HOS 1 + 2)
Next:     sweep a heater stuck on during a jam, 10 seeds (HOS 4)
Blocked:  Grafana + InfluxDB + training run out of memory together — train offline, load weights

Minimum bar

Outcome CO6

A project that meets the course outcome does all of this:

  • Real-time monitoring: live (or replayed) sensor data flows over MQTT into the virtual entity, with a dashboard and at least one data-quality check (HOS 1).
  • A physics-informed model the twin actually uses for a prediction, with evidence it beats a data-only model where the twin needs it (HOS 2).
  • A simulation or synthetic-data piece that trains or tests part of the twin, tested on conditions it wasn't built from (HOS 3).
  • At least one autonomous decision, made only inside a safety envelope derived from what-if tests (including a combined failure), with a stop-and-alert condition and every proposal logged (HOS 4).
  • It runs in a Codespace from the README, with no paid service and no committed keys.
  • A README someone could read in 3 minutes: the use case, an architecture diagram of the three components, what the twin may decide and what it may not, one failure and how it was fixed, the AI Tool Disclosure, and a link to the demo video.

Self-check and reflection

Feeds your Author Contribution statement

Self-check: can you explain, without notes, how each of the 4 HOS shows up in your twin, what the twin may decide on its own and why, and where it would stop and ask a person? Say it out loud once before the demo.

Reflection prompts

  • Each member: what you personally built (link commits or issues), one decision you argued for, and one thing a teammate did that made the project better. Solo: what you built, one decision you'd make differently, and one piece of feedback (instructor or classmate) that changed the project.
  • Everyone: one moment where a test, a what-if or the action log caught a problem before it reached the demo.

Your final report ends with an Author Contribution statement detailing each member's weekly tasks (solo: your own weekly tasks); these notes are its raw material. Full description: the Term Project section of the syllabus (in Brightspace).
