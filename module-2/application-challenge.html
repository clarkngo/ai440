Application Challenge 2: A bakery oven that cools impossibly

A bakery's oven-cooling model fits its measurements and predicts physically impossible temperatures between and beyond them. Add the cooling law to its training loss, decide how much the physics should weigh when the measurements disagree with it, and explain where a physics-informed model still shouldn't be trusted.

Ground rules

Everything here runs in GitHub Codespaces on CPU, on free tools, with no credit card and no API key. You may use an AI assistant for any part; say what it did in DECISIONS.md. See the AI Use Guide.

Starter code: Planned The AC 2 starter repository and its GitHub Classroom invite are being built and tested; the invite link goes here when they are published.

🥖

The scenario

Rainier Bakehouse (fictional) switches its deck oven off at 230 °C after the last bake, and wants to know when it will be down to 60 °C, the temperature at which overnight proofing trays can go in on the top deck. A thermocouple logged the first 20 minutes of cooling, every 2 minutes, in a 24 °C kitchen. The model a contractor left behind fits those 11 points well, then predicts the oven dipping below room temperature at minute 70 and warming again by minute 100.

The starter has the readings (data/cooldown.csv), the contractor's data-only network (oven/data_only.py) and an empty oven/pinn.py. The law is Newton's law of cooling, dT/dt = −k (T − Troom), with k unknown. Two things to know:

  • Everything runs on CPU, in plain PyTorch, in under two minutes per training run.
  • One stretch of the readings breaks the law: someone opened the oven door for two minutes around minute 12, and the temperature dropped faster than any single k explains.
📐

The spec

Every requirement below is checked by a provided test.

PartMust doMust not do
train(readings, lam, epochs=3000, seed=0)Trains a small network T(t) with a data term, lam × a physics term (the cooling-law residual at collocation points from 0 to 180 minutes) and the initial condition T(0) = 230 °C. Learns k as a positive parameter.Compute the physics term only at the measurement times, or let k go negative.
predict(model, t) and learned_k(model)Return temperatures at any time up to 180 minutes, and the learned k.Predict below the kitchen's temperature, or a rise at any point.
time_to(model, temp)Returns the minute the prediction first reaches temp (the bakery asks for 60 °C).Return a time outside the predicted range without saying so.

The open decision: how much should the physics weigh?

The head baker's only instruction: "Don't tell me the oven's ready when it isn't; proofing dough at 80 °C kills it." The door-opening stretch disagrees with the law. A heavy physics weight ignores it; a light one lets it bend the whole curve. The provided tests only check that the model is physical and close to the reference at 60 and 120 minutes, for one λ of your choosing. You decide the weight (and whether to treat those readings differently), prove it with your own tests, and defend it in DECISIONS.md.

🚦

Where shouldn't it be trusted?

You don't build these. In your memo, decide for each situation whether the bakery can trust your model's answer, and what would have to change (the data, the law, or a person checking).

  1. The oven door is opened halfway through cooling to pull a forgotten tray.
  2. The bakery buys a second, larger oven of a different model.
  3. The convection fan is left running after switch-off.
  4. The baker wants the temperature at the back of the oven, far from the thermocouple.
  5. The kitchen is 35 °C on a summer night.
🪜

Steps & what to submit

1Open it and run the tests

Open your own AC 2 repo in GitHub Codespaces, then run:

pytest

The physics tests fail on the contractor's model, and oven/pinn.py raises NotImplementedError.

2Build the physics-informed model

Fill in oven/pinn.py until the provided tests pass. You may use an AI assistant; tell it to work in this existing workspace, CPU only, and not to change the tests, and read what it writes: you'll defend it.

3Make the physics-weight call

Choose your λ (and any handling of the door readings). Write at least two tests in tests/test_my_rules.py: one for your rule, and one for the edge you think is most likely to go wrong.

4Run it at two weights

Train with your weight and with one extreme, and plot both:

python -m oven.compare --lam YOUR_LAMBDA --lam 1000

Add both plots and the printed time_to(60) and k to RUNS.md.

5Write the memo

Fill in DECISIONS.md, about one page: your physics weight and why, what happens when the door is opened mid-cool, your call on each of the five situations, and what your AI assistant wrote, got wrong or missed.

Submit (in Brightspace)

  • oven/pinn.py and tests/test_my_rules.py
  • The output of pytest, all tests passing (including yours)
  • RUNS.md: your chosen weight and one extreme weight, each with its plot
  • DECISIONS.md: your physics weight and why, what happens when the door is opened mid-cool, and where the model shouldn't be trusted

How this differs from HOS 2's Understand & Refine: there you extended code you already understood, by hand, with no AI. Here the business, the code and the decisions are new, AI help is allowed, and half the grade (Analysis and Evaluation) is for the calls you make, not the typing.

🧯

Common snags

  • The learned k goes negative or explodes — learn log_k and use k = log_k.exp(), and start it from a sensible guess (the starter's K_GUESS).
  • Training is unstable — scale time to [0, 1] over 180 minutes and temperature to roughly [0, 1], and rescale k with time.
  • test_never_below_room fails late in the curve — your collocation points stop at minute 20. Sample them over the whole 0–180 minutes.
  • The assistant changed a test to make it pass — undo it with git checkout tests/. The provided tests are the spec.
