What's the HOS?

HOS 1 (Hands-On Skill) is this module's main build and 10% of your course grade. It has four stages across the two weeks: Create / Scaffold and Evaluate in Week 1, then Analyze and Understand & Refine in Week 2. All four go in one GitHub Classroom repo, and that repo is your submission: there's nothing to upload in Brightspace, and your last push before the end of Week 2 is what counts. The Muddiest Point, Concept Debate, Application Challenge and Knowledge Check are graded separately.

A dashboard that shows a building's temperatures is useful. A system that reads those temperatures and changes the heating is a different kind of thing: now a wrong reading can do something to the physical world. That difference, one arrow from the virtual side back to the physical side, is the first idea of this course, and this HOS makes it concrete on one floor of a building.

Week 1 builds the data connection: four simulated rooms publish temperature, CO₂ and occupancy over MQTT; Mosquitto, Node-RED, InfluxDB and Grafana carry, route, store and show them; and a virtual model you build with an AI assistant keeps each room's live state. Then you measure what "real-time" actually means in that pipeline, and break it on purpose. Week 2 is the harder half: explaining one mechanism line by line, and writing the data-quality check that decides when the virtual model must stop trusting a sensor, by hand.

Anchoring analogy

A flight-tracking website and air traffic control see the same planes. The website is a digital shadow: live positions, flowing one way, and nobody's life depends on it being a few seconds late. Air traffic control can tell a plane to change course, so it's a twin: the same data plus one arrow back, and suddenly latency, stale data and a lying transponder all matter a great deal more.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between a digital shadow and a digital twin, with an example of each from your own build. CO1
  2. Design a twin's three components (physical entity, virtual entity and data connection) for a real system. CO1
  3. Develop a real-time pipeline that keeps a virtual model in sync with streaming sensor data. CO3
  4. Measure end-to-end latency and data loss, hop by hop, and decide what "real-time" must mean for a use case. CO3
  5. Detect stuck, impossible and missing readings before they reach the virtual model. CO3

Lecture: from a physical thing to a live virtual copy

≈ 60 min3 short partsCO1 · CO3

Three short segments: read the notes, then try the quick check before moving on. The notes cover everything you need; the "Read more" links are optional. If something stays fuzzy, that's exactly what your Muddiest Point is for.

Part 1 — Shadows, twins and the three components ≈ 20 min

A digital model is a copy that someone updates by hand, such as a CAD drawing. A digital shadow updates itself: data flows automatically from the physical thing to the virtual one, but nothing flows back. A digital twin adds the return path: the virtual side can act on the physical side, by changing a setpoint, slowing a motor or opening a vent.

Digital modelDigital shadowDigital twin
Physical → virtualBy handAutomatic, liveAutomatic, live
Virtual → physicalNoneNone (a person may act on what they see)Automatic: the twin can act
Floor 3 exampleA floor plan with room sizesA live Grafana dashboard of the four roomsA model that turns a room's heating down when it predicts overheating

Every twin, however large, has the same three components, named in Outcome CO1: the physical entity (the rooms and their sensors and heaters), the virtual entity (the model that holds each room's current state, and later predicts and decides), and the data connection between them (the pipeline you build this week, plus the control path).

Worked example

Room 3's sensor reads 24.8 °C. In a shadow, that number appears on a dashboard and a facilities manager may or may not notice it. In a twin, the virtual entity compares it with the 24 °C setpoint and publishes "heating off" to building/floor3/r3/hvac/set. Same reading, same pipeline. The only new code is the control path, and it changes what a bad reading can do.

Read more (optional): Kritzinger et al., Digital Twin in manufacturing: A categorical literature review and classification (2018), the paper that popularized the model / shadow / twin distinction.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Calling any dashboard a twin. Live data alone makes a shadow. A twin needs the arrow back.
  • Treating the virtual entity as "the database". InfluxDB stores history. The virtual entity holds the current state and, later, the model that predicts and decides.
  • Adding the arrow before the data can be trusted. Control multiplies the cost of every bad reading. That's why this HOS measures and breaks the pipeline before anything controls a heater.
Quick check

A dashboard emails a technician when a room is above 26 °C. Is it a shadow or a twin? What would you have to add to make it a twin, and what could then go wrong that couldn't before?

Part 2 — The data connection: MQTT to dashboard ≈ 20 min

Sensors publish small messages to topics on an MQTT broker; anything interested subscribes. The sensor never knows who is listening, which is why you can add a dashboard, a database writer and a virtual model without touching the sensor code.

Sensorsphysical entity · sensors/simulate.py
→
MosquittoMQTT broker
→
Node-REDroutes and validates
→
InfluxDBtime-series store
→
Grafanalive dashboard

Topics are paths: building/floor3/r2/temp. Wildcards let one subscriber cover many sensors: building/floor3/+/temp is every room's temperature, and building/floor3/# is everything on the floor. Each payload carries its own sensor timestamp and a sequence number:

topic:   building/floor3/r2/temp
payload: {"v": 21.4, "unit": "C", "ts": "2027-01-11T09:00:02.140Z", "seq": 1042}

InfluxDB stores each reading as a point: a measurement (temp), tags you filter by (room=r2), a field (v=21.4) and a timestamp. Grafana queries it every few seconds. The virtual entity, which you build this week, subscribes to the broker directly, because it needs the latest state, not history.

Read more (optional): mqtt.org · Mosquitto documentation · InfluxDB documentation · Node-RED documentation

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Stamping readings when they arrive. Then you can't measure latency, and a message delayed by a broker restart looks fresh. Keep the sensor's own timestamp.
  • One topic for everything. Every subscriber then has to parse every message. Put the room and the measurement in the topic.
  • Assuming MQTT delivers everything. At QoS 0 a message can be lost; even at QoS 1 it can arrive twice. The sequence number is how you notice.
Quick check

Why does each payload carry its own timestamp and sequence number, when the broker and InfluxDB both record when a message arrived?

Part 3 — What "real-time" means, and bad data ≈ 20 min

"Real-time" is a number, not a feeling: the delay from a physical reading to its effect in the virtual model, plus how much data is lost on the way. Measure it per hop (sensor → broker → Node-RED → InfluxDB → dashboard, and sensor → broker → virtual entity) and look at the worst case, not the average. A thermostat tolerates seconds; a drone's position does not.

Sensors also fail in recognizable ways, and the worst ones look like real data:

FaultWhat you seeHow you could catch it
Stuck valueThe same reading, over and over, while the room changesNo change for longer than the physics allows
Impossible jump21 °C, then 85 °C two seconds laterA rate of change no room can reach
DropoutNothing at allThe reading's age grows past a limit
Clock skewReadings stamped minutes in the past or futureCompare the sensor timestamp with arrival time
Worked example

Room 2's sensor freezes at 21.4 °C for an hour while the sun heats the room to 27 °C. The dashboard looks calm. A "no change for ten minutes" rule flags it. If the twin had been controlling the heating, it would have kept the heat on the whole time, because as far as it knew, the room was cool.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Reporting the average delay. The average hides the slow tail. Report the median and the worst case.
  • Treating a missing reading as zero, or as "no change". Missing means unknown. The virtual entity should say so.
  • Validating only in the dashboard. If the check lives in Grafana, the virtual entity, and anything it controls, still sees the bad value.
Quick check

A smart thermostat and a delivery drone both stream position or temperature over MQTT. What latency does each tolerate, and what should each do when its data is a few seconds stale?

▶ Rehearse before the build: Open the Sensor Pipeline Simulator. Inject a stuck sensor with twin mode off, then on; then raise the per-hop delay.

    Side A — Let it control, within limitsGroup 1 this HOS

    Close the loop: the twin acts automatically inside tested limits.

    • People can't react in milliseconds; production lines and drones need automation.
    • Operators stop reading advice they see a hundred times a day.
    • Limits and logging make automatic control auditable.

    Side B — Advise onlyGroup 2 this HOS

    The twin recommends; a named person decides and acts.

    • Sensor faults look like real readings; a person can sanity-check.
    • Physical damage can't be rolled back.
    • Accountability stays with someone who can be asked why.

    Example — a packaging cell

HOS 1 streams the conveyor speed, the sealer's jaw temperature and a light curtain over MQTT; HOS 2 predicts the jaw temperature between its two sensors; HOS 3 simulates the arm and generates images to train its pick detector; HOS 4 tests what happens when the sealer's heater sticks on during a jam, and lets an agent slow the conveyor only inside the limits those tests proved.
