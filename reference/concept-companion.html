Module 1: Digital Shadows, Twins & Real-Time Pipelines

HOS 1CO1 · CO3

Why before you start: A twin is only as good as the data reaching it. Everything later (physics, simulation, autonomy) sits on top of this pipeline, so its delays and its faults become theirs.

The data connection, one hop at a time. Without the arrow back it is a digital shadow.

Digital shadow vs. digital twin

A digital shadow is a live virtual copy fed automatically from the physical system: data flows one way. A digital twin adds the arrow back: the virtual side acts on the physical one (a setpoint, a speed, a valve).

Why it matters: CO1 asks you to tell them apart and design their parts. One arrow is the whole difference, and it brings a new failure: a wrong reading becomes a wrong action.

The three components

The physical entity (the room and its sensors), the virtual entity (the model that holds its state), and the data connection between them (broker, routing, storage, dashboard).

Why it matters: Every design question in this course is about one of these three or the arrows between them.

MQTT: publish and subscribe

Sensors publish messages to topics such as building/floor3/r2/temp; anything that subscribes to a topic gets them, through a broker. Wildcards: + matches one level, # the rest. QoS 0, 1 and 2 trade speed for delivery guarantees.

Why it matters: Publishers and subscribers never know about each other, so you can add a twin, a dashboard or a logger without touching the sensors.

Timestamps and sequence numbers

Each payload carries the sensor's own time (ts) and a counter (seq). Arrival time minus ts is the delay; a gap in seq is a lost message.

Why it matters: Without them, a late or missing reading looks exactly like a current one.

"Real-time" is a number

The delay a use case can tolerate: a room's heating copes with a minute, a drone with milliseconds. Measure each hop, and decide what is fast enough for this decision.

Why it matters: CO3 asks for pipelines that synchronize sensors with virtual models. "Synchronized" means within a delay you chose and can defend.

Data-quality faults

Stuck (the same value forever), spikes (physically impossible jumps), dropout (nothing arrives) and stale (the newest value is old). The stuck sensor is the dangerous one: it looks calm and normal.

Why it matters: Your Week 2 build decides when the twin stops believing a sensor, and what it does instead.

Vocabulary

Topic
the address an MQTT message is published to.
Broker
the server that passes messages from publishers to subscribers.
QoS
MQTT's delivery guarantee: at most once, at least once, exactly once.
Time-series database
storage indexed by time, such as InfluxDB.
Latency
the delay from the sensor's reading to its use.

Quick self-check before you continue

  • What exactly turns a digital shadow into a digital twin?
  • Why is a stuck sensor more dangerous than one that stops sending?
  • Why does each message carry the sensor's own timestamp?
Check your thinking
  1. The virtual side acting on the physical one automatically: the arrow back. A dashboard that alerts a person is still a shadow.
  2. A silent sensor is obvious: its data gets old. A stuck one keeps sending a believable value, so nothing looks wrong unless something checks.
  3. So the receiver can measure the delay and spot late or out-of-order data. Arrival time alone hides both.

Module 2: Physics-Informed Neural Networks

HOS 2CO2

Why before you start: A twin is consulted where there are no sensors: inside a wall, between probes, an hour from now. A model that only fits the data can do anything there. Physics is the information you have in those gaps.

Three kinds of evidence in one loss: what was measured, the law, and what is known at the edges.

Why data-only models break physics

With few, noisy readings a flexible model fits the noise and extrapolates however its shape continues. It can predict a wall colder than the air outside it.

Why it matters: Judge models between the sensors and past the data, where the twin is used, not at the sensors.

The governing law

Heat in a wall with no source: ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x², where α is the material's diffusivity. The maximum principle: nothing inside gets colder or hotter than its boundaries and starting values. Other laws in CO2: x″ = −g for a falling object, m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0 for a damped spring.

Why it matters: A prediction that breaks the law isn't just inaccurate; it's impossible.

The PINN loss

A data term at the readings, a physics term (the squared residual of the equation) weighted by λ, and boundary and initial terms. λ = 0 gives a data-only network; a huge λ ignores the data.

Why it matters: λ is a design choice you will tune and defend.

Derivatives by automatic differentiation

torch.autograd.grad gives the exact derivative of the network's output with respect to its inputs. Taking it twice (for ∂²u/∂x²) needs create_graph=True on the first.

Why it matters: The most common silent bug: a broken graph makes the physics term do nothing.

Collocation points

Random points over the whole domain (all of the wall, all twelve hours) where the physics term is computed, with or without a reading there. The residual is compared with zero: the law should hold everywhere.

Why it matters: This is the most common Muddiest Point in this module. If it isn't clear yet, it's a good one to ask.

Confidently wrong, and the fix

A wrong parameter (a spec-sheet α) gives a smooth, confident, wrong prediction. Making α a learnable parameter lets the data correct it; it can't fix a wrong equation.

Why it matters: Your Week 4 build, by hand.

Vocabulary

Residual
how far a prediction is from satisfying the equation at a point.
Collocation point
a point where the physics term is computed.
Diffusivity (α)
how fast a material spreads heat.
Boundary condition
a known value at the edge of the domain.
DeepXDE
a library for PINNs, used to compare with your own.

Quick self-check before you continue

  • What is your PINN when λ = 0?
  • Why is the physics term computed at random points with no readings?
  • Name a problem where a PINN is the wrong choice.
Check your thinking
  1. A data-only network: nothing in the loss mentions the physics.
  2. Because the law must hold everywhere, especially where the twin has no data. There the residual is compared with zero, not with a reading.
  3. One with no governing law (customer foot traffic), or a known equation with no data at all, where a classical solver is simpler.

Module 3: Simulation, Synthetic Data & Sim-to-Real

HOS 3CO4

Why before you start: Physical AI is trained in simulation because the real thing is slow, costly and sometimes dangerous. The skill is making what's learned there survive contact with a world that's a bit different.

Train across varied worlds, then test on worlds the policy never saw.

Why simulate

Speed (thousands of episodes an hour), safety (crashes cost nothing) and perfect labels (the simulator knows where everything is). Rare events can be practiced as often as common ones.

Why it matters: Synthetic data is the only practical way to train on the jam that happens once a month.

The sim-to-real gap

Every way the simulator differs from reality: estimated parameters, approximate contact, clean sensors, clean images. A policy can learn to exploit any of them.

Why it matters: Success in the training world says the policy learned the simulator.

MuJoCo, OpenUSD and BlenderProc

MuJoCo steps the physics from an XML model (MJCF). OpenUSD is a shared scene format many tools read. BlenderProc renders images and writes their labels.

Why it matters: Three tools, three jobs: physics, description, pictures. The skills transfer to larger platforms such as Isaac Sim.

Physically accurate vs. photorealistic

Accurate: the numbers are right (mass, friction, motor strength). Photorealistic: the images look like camera frames. A pushing policy needs the first and no images; a detector needs varied images more than beautiful ones.

Why it matters: On two CPU cores, realism is a budget. Spend it on what the task depends on.

Domain randomization

Each episode draws new values from a range, so the policy can't rely on any one. Too narrow and reality falls outside; too wide and nothing is learned.

Why it matters: The ranges are the design decision. Write down where each one came from.

Held-out worlds

Test worlds with altered physics, some inside the training ranges and some outside. Without hardware, they stand in for reality, with one limit: they share the simulator's blind spots.

Why it matters: Report every world, not just the average.

Vocabulary

MJCF
MuJoCo's XML model format.
Episode
one attempt at the task, from reset to end.
PPO
Proximal Policy Optimization, a reinforcement-learning algorithm.
System identification
measuring real parameters to set in the simulator.
Synthetic data
data generated by a simulator or renderer, labels included.

Quick self-check before you continue

  • A policy scores 98% in its own world and 41% on a damp carton. What happened?
  • Why can a very wide randomization range be worse than none?
  • What can held-out worlds not show you?
Check your thinking
  1. It learned the simulator's one carton, not pushing. The damp carton is outside everything it trained on.
  2. If no single behavior works across the whole range, the policy can't learn one, and learns to do almost nothing.
  3. Anything the simulator itself can't represent, such as a contact effect it models wrongly. Real data is the only test for that.

Module 4: What-If Testing & Safe Autonomous Decisions

HOS 4CO5

Why before you start: The point of a twin is that you can break it instead of the building. What-if tests tell you where the system fails; the envelope turns that into limits an autonomous agent can't cross.

Every limit in the envelope should point back to a sweep result.

What-if scenarios and sweeps

A what-if is a question asked of the twin instead of the building. A sweep runs every combination of a few factors at a few values, with several random seeds each, and maps where the system fails.

Why it matters: CO5: predict failures and assess autonomous actions before deployment.

Combined failures

Conditions that are each survivable and fail together: a heat wave, a failed unit and a full floor. Single-scenario tests miss them by design.

Why it matters: Real incidents are usually combinations. Sweep the factors together.

SimPy

A discrete-event simulation library: processes (a room heating, a unit cooling) and events at set times (a failure at 14:00). Each run takes milliseconds, so hundreds fit in a few minutes on CPU.

Why it matters: Fast enough to sweep; simple enough to read.

The safety envelope

Three kinds of rule: state limits (a setpoint range), rate limits (how often and how far), and stop-and-alert conditions (when the twin no longer matches the building).

Why it matters: Too tight is useless; too loose is unsafe. Count what each limit costs.

Rules in code, not in the prompt

In ADK a before_tool_callback runs before every tool call. Returning None allows it; returning a dict skips the tool and hands that dict back as its result.

Why it matters: An instruction is a request; a callback is a rule the agent's reasoning can't talk past.

Accountability and the action log

Autonomy is a dial: advise, act with approval, act inside the envelope, act freely. When something goes wrong, the log (inputs, twin state, proposal, verdict, reason) shows which layer failed.

Why it matters: Without the log, "who is accountable?" has no answer.

Vocabulary

Seed
the starting value for a run's randomness; several seeds show the spread.
Envelope
the states and actions tests showed to be safe.
Stop-and-alert
halting autonomy and telling a person.
Callback
code ADK runs at a fixed point in every run.
Autonomy level
how much of an action the system may do alone.

Quick self-check before you continue

  • Why does a sweep of single failures miss the worst afternoon?
  • Where should the safety limits live, and why?
  • An envelope is correct and a room still overheats. How?
Check your thinking
  1. Because the worst case is a combination, and a single-failure sweep never runs one.
  2. In code outside the agent, such as a before_tool_callback, so no instruction or reasoning can get past them.
  3. The data under it was wrong (a stuck sensor), or the situation was outside anything the sweep tested. The envelope inherits its model's blind spots.

Putting it together: all 4 HOS

Before the Week 8 reviewCO6

Why before you start: The Term Project adds no new concepts. It asks you to connect the four you have, and to know where the connections fail.

One reading, one action: the loop your Term Project has to close.
HOSWhat it adds to the twinHow it fails silentlyWhat catches it
HOS 1Live, synchronized data and the arrow backA stuck sensor that looks calmData-quality checks; stop-and-alert
HOS 2Predictions where there are no sensorsA wrong parameter: smooth and wrongComparing with readings; a learnable parameter
HOS 3Training and testing without the real systemA simulator blind spotHeld-out worlds; a little real data
HOS 4Limits for autonomy, and a record of itAn untested combinationSweeps with combined failures; the action log

Errors travel down the loop

A stuck sensor (HOS 1) makes the PINN's input wrong (HOS 2), which makes the envelope's view of the state wrong (HOS 4). Each layer can be correct and the whole loop still wrong.

Why it matters: the Integration Debate asks which is the bigger risk: an inaccurate model, or autonomy on an accurate one.

Narrow autonomy, complete evidence

A strong Term Project lets the twin take one small, reversible action, inside limits its what-if tests support, with a stop-and-alert rule and every proposal logged.

Why it matters: CO6 asks for real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making. The second is only credible with the evidence behind it.

Quick self-check before the Week 8 review

  • Can you draw your twin's loop from memory and label each arrow with its HOS?
  • Which step in your loop would fail without anyone noticing, and what would catch it?
  • Which one action may your twin take on its own, and which sweep result supports it?
