The setup

HOS 1 · Week 1CO1

The question (already in the Module 1 lesson): Should a digital twin ever control the physical system directly, or only advise a human operator?

  • Side A — Let it control, within limits. Close the loop: the twin acts automatically inside tested limits.
  • Side B — Advise only. The twin recommends; a named person decides and acts.

Before the first debate, the class is split into two debate groups of about the same size, either assigned by the instructor or chosen by students. In HOS 1, Debate Group 1 argues Side A and Debate Group 2 argues Side B. The four students below are fictional.

Timeline

Week 1 of the HOSInstructor steps in purple

Days follow the suggested pacing in the lessons; your Brightspace dates win.

Week 1 · Monday

The topic opens

After the lecture, students read the question and both sides in the lesson. The instructor posts nothing new; the question is already in the course.

Monday · instructor

Check the groups

Confirm the two debate groups are close to even, and place anyone who added late.

Monday – Tuesday

Stage 1: initial position

Each student starts their own thread, titled with their side and name. Until they post it, Brightspace hides everyone else's threads.

Wednesday · instructor

Skim Stage 1 and nudge

About 15 minutes. Look for topic summaries with no claim or evidence, and leave one short public question on those threads. Note anyone who hasn't posted.

Wednesday – Thursday

Stage 2: argue the opposite

Each student replies to their own thread with the strongest case for the side they didn't argue. Now they can read everyone's posts.

Friday – Saturday

Stage 3: synthesis

Each student replies to a classmate's thread from the other side, citing one specific point from it. The topic closes Saturday night.

Week 2 · Monday · instructor

Grade and reuse

Grade from the topic's Assessment view (about 2 minutes per student; each student's posts appear together). Quote one strong synthesis, anonymized or with permission, in the Week 2 lecture.

The posts

In the order they were written

Stage 1 · Monday – Tuesday: initial position

Side A — MeiSide AStage 1Mon 8:40 pm

Claim: a twin should control slow, reversible actions itself, because a person reading its advice adds nothing when they see the same data it does.

Evidence: in the Sensor Pipeline Simulator I set the fault to stuck with validation off. In advise mode the dashboard showed a calm 21.4 °C for twenty readings while the room cooled, and the "operator" (me) would have agreed with it. The person was no check at all. What caught it was turning on validation: a rule in code, which works the same in either mode.

Why it works: one claim, and evidence from a run she actually did, with the setting she used.
Side B — DevSide BStage 1Tue 10:15 am

Claim: a twin should only advise, because physical damage can't be rolled back the way software can.

Evidence: in my HOS 1 Week 2 build, I measured my pipeline's delay from ts to the twin at 1.2 s on a quiet Codespace, and over 6 s while Grafana was rebuilding a panel. A twin acting on a 6-second-old reading is acting on a building that no longer exists. A person can wait for the next reading; a valve that already opened can't un-open.

Why it works: a real number from his own build, tied to why the action matters.
Side A — TomásSide AStage 1Tue 9:50 pm

A digital twin is a virtual copy of a physical system connected by real-time data. Unlike a digital shadow, a twin can send commands back to the physical system. This is the future of Industry 4.0 and makes factories more efficient.

What's missing: this is a summary of what a twin is, not an argument for when it should control. No specific claim, no evidence.
InstructorInstructorWed 9:10 am

Tomás, this explains what a twin is, but not why it should act instead of advising. For Stage 2, pick one specific system and say what goes wrong if a person has to approve every action there.

Instructor move: one public question, not a grade. Other students can see what a stronger post needs.
Side B — RosaSide BStage 1Wed 7:30 am

Claim: a twin should only advise, because accountability needs a person who can be asked why.

Evidence: the lecture's water-utility example: when an automated setpoint change went wrong, nobody could say which reading it had acted on, because nothing was logged. With an operator in the loop there is always someone who decided, and a record of when.

Strong, but late: posted a day after the Stage 1 deadline. That costs Participation, not Writing.

Stage 2 · Wednesday – Thursday: argue the opposite (a reply to your own thread)

MeiSide AStage 2Wed 6:05 pm

The strongest case for advise only: my own run cuts both ways. Validation caught the stuck sensor, but only the fault I wrote a rule for. A twin in control acts on every fault nobody anticipated, at machine speed, with nobody watching. An advising twin with a wrong reading costs a bad recommendation; a controlling one costs whatever it was controlling.

DevSide BStage 2Thu 2:30 pm

The strongest case for control: on a packaging line a jam has to stop the conveyor in under a second, and no person approves that in time. And people do stop reading advice: an operator who clicks "accept" on the hundredth identical recommendation of the day isn't a check any more, just a slower actuator.

TomásSide AStage 2Thu 11:05 pm

The other side would say that a person is safer than automation.

What's missing: names the other side's point but doesn't make its strongest case, and doesn't take up the instructor's nudge.
RosaSide BStage 2Thu 8:00 pm

The strongest case for control: my accountability point is about the log, not about who acts. A twin that records the reading, its state, the proposed action and the limit it checked is more accountable than an operator who clicked accept at 3 a.m. and doesn't remember why.

Stage 3 · Friday – Saturday: synthesis (a reply to a classmate from the other side)

Mei → Dev's threadSide AStage 3Fri 7:45 pm

Dev's 6-second delay changed where I draw the line. My stuck-sensor point still stands, but a twin that controls needs to know how old its data is, and to stop when the data is too old for the action. My position: let the twin control slow, reversible actions such as a heating setpoint, only while its newest reading is younger than a limit chosen for that action; past that limit it falls back to advising.

AI use: I asked Copilot for the best objection to my position. It said latency limits are hard to choose, which is why I tied mine to a measured delay instead of a guess.

Why it works: cites a specific point from Dev, changes her own view because of it, says how AI was used, and lands on a position that isn't just "it depends".
Dev → Mei's threadSide BStage 3Sat 11:20 am

Mei's stuck-sensor run convinced me that "a person checks it" isn't a safeguard if the person sees the same bad data. I'm dropping "advise only, always". My position: data-quality checks come first in either mode, and the twin may act only where the action is reversible within one reading interval. Anything irreversible stays advice-only.

Rosa → Tomás's threadSide BStage 3Sat 4:10 pm

Building on Tomás's point that a twin sends commands back: each of those commands is a message we can log, which answers my own accountability worry from Stage 1. So the choice isn't "a person or a machine". My synthesis: a twin can control what it can explain afterwards, from its log, and a person should decide whatever the log couldn't justify.

Why it works: finds the one usable point in a weak post and builds on it, which counts as engaging with a classmate.

Great points! I agree both sides have pros and cons and it really depends on the system.

AI use in a debate

AI can help you think, not write

You may use an AI assistant to test your thinking. Your posts must be your own words and your own reasoning. Here's the difference in practice, on this debate's question:

Pasted AI text"Digital twins offer significant benefits for automation, while human oversight ensures safety. The optimal approach balances autonomy and control based on the specific use case."

What's missing: no classmate's point, no specific evidence, and no position. It would earn little under Writing without anyone needing to ask where it came from.

Set it up in Brightspace

Instructor · once per term, then once per debate
  1. One forum, "Concept Debates", with one topic per debate: HOS 1–4 and the Week 8 Integration debate. Paste the question and both sides into each topic's description.
  2. Turn on "Users must start a thread before they can read and reply to other threads in this topic" (Post and View settings). Nobody can read and borrow arguments before posting their own Stage 1.
  3. Set the topic's end date to the Stage 3 deadline. Brightspace allows one end date per topic, so check Stage 1 and Stage 2 times when grading rather than splitting a debate into three topics.
  4. Assessment tab: score out of 10 and attach the Participation 50 / Writing 50 rubric, so you can grade from the topic with each student's posts together.
  5. Tell students the thread title format: "Side A — First name L." It makes the topic easy to scan.
  6. Keep it one whole-class topic. Above about 30–35 students, use Brightspace groups to make two or three restricted copies of the topic, each with a mix of both sides. Don't split by side: Stages 2 and 3 need the other side's posts.

Not one thread for everyone: replies nest until they're unreadable, the start-a-thread-first setting stops working, and grading means hunting for each student's posts.
